A faster answer is not the same as learning.

Generative AI changes both how secondary-school students study and how they show what they know. The educational question is not simply whether a tool was used. It is what remains when the tool is put away.

01

Output is visible. Learning is latent.

Generative AI can make a student’s work look stronger in the moment. That visible improvement is not, by itself, proof that the student has learned the underlying skill.

Two moments. Two different measures.

During practice, an AI tool can supply a next step, explanation, or polished answer. But learning is tested later, when the support is gone and a student must retrieve, adapt, and explain an idea independently. In a high-school mathematics field experiment, students with unrestricted GPT-4 support did better during practice, then scored below students who had never used the tool on a later unaided exam. A tutor designed to give teacher-guided hints largely avoided that decline.

The beginner’s disadvantage

A student who is new to a topic has less knowledge to use as a filter. A plausible explanation may contain a logical error, an unsupported claim, or a missing step, yet still sound convincing. If a learner has only practised the interface, they may not know what to challenge. Foundations matter: they give students something to compare an AI answer against.

“

The goal is not to remove AI from school. It is to help students master the basics without it—and then use it critically.

THE EDUCATIONAL DESIGN QUESTION

Build the task around the thinking

The same tool can short-circuit practice or make a useful tutor. The difference is often in the task: does AI hand over the finished answer, or does it offer a bounded hint that leaves the student to do the important work? A teacher can make checking part of the assignment, asking students to test an output, explain a choice, or identify where a model’s reasoning fails.

Assessment should reveal learning

Because AI use cannot always be reliably detected, suspicion is a weak foundation for assessment. Instead, design tasks that make learning visible. Check essential knowledge in class without AI through a brief written response or oral explanation. For longer work, look at the process: drafts, decisions, and the student’s account of how the final result took shape.

Good AI use is not a property of the tool. It emerges from the task, the learner’s foundations, and the teacher’s guidance.
